# The Pitch Spine

The structure every pitch fills, distilled from three decades of startup and product-innovation practice
(the CSEP 595 / ENTRE 592 lineage, plus Strategyzer/ODI-style customer discovery). It's product-agnostic:
the same beats work for a startup raising money, a team pitching an exec, or a founder aligning cofounders.

Fill these slots from the product's substance. Where the substance is silent, flag `[needs data]` — do
not invent. The **why** under each beat matters more than the label; understand it and you can adapt the
order when a specific pitch calls for it.

## The core question order

A pitch answers questions in the order the audience actually asks them (follow the "why"):

1. **Why am I listening?** — hook them before anything else.
2. **Why does anyone care?** — the problem, made real.
3. **What is it?** — the product, as jobs done, not features.
4. **Who's it for?** — the customer, specifically.
5. **How do you reach them?** — distribution.
6. **How does it make money / what's the ask?**
7. **Why you? Why win?** — team and durable advantage.
8. **What's the aspiration?** — the future you're buying into.

Everything below hangs on this skeleton.

## The seven beats (+ two lenses over all of them)

### 1. The problem — specific, durable, personal
- **Specific problem for a specific person.** Not "communication is broken" but a named person hitting a
  named wall. Laser focus; abandonment of vague problems is brutal.
- **Durable.** Will it still be a problem in ~20 years? Investors and execs fund durable pain, not
  momentary gaps. Say *why* it persists.
- **Personal.** Why *this* problem, for *this* founder/team? If it's someone else's problem you'll quit
  when it's hard. This is often the one paragraph only the founder can write — mark it `[needs data]` if
  the source doesn't contain it.
- **The knowledge/permission distinction and the like** — dig for the *real* barrier. Often the obvious
  framing ("people can't do X") is wrong and the true blocker is subtler ("people can do X but can't
  know *how*"). The sharper the diagnosis, the stronger the pitch.

### 2. Why now — the inflection
Name the recent shift(s) that make this possible or urgent *today* and not three years ago: a technology
that crossed a threshold, a behavior that changed, a cost that collapsed. "God creates markets; we just
take share." If there's a clock (a deadline, a closing window), show it.

### 3. The product — jobs, not a platform
- **Don't pitch a platform; pitch jobs to be done.** List the handful of jobs the customer needs done and
  what each gets them. Resist listing capabilities/architecture.
- **Benefits, not features.** Translate every feature into the outcome it buys.
- **The back of the box** (write this early — it clarifies everything else): the short, punchy promise as
  if you were writing the product's packaging or a press-release headline. If you can't write it, you
  don't yet understand the product.
- **The novel/unique mechanism.** What's genuinely new here? State it plainly; it's the seed of "why win."

### 4. The customer — be specific; often two buyers
- **Demographics + psychographics** (how they think, what they value, what they do). Describe them
  concretely enough to picture one.
- **Sell the same thing to two different people** when the buyer ≠ the user (e.g. the builder who gets
  *speed* and the boss who gets *proof*). Name both and what each gets.
- **You can't pursue everyone — pick a beachhead.** The sharpest, most acute instance of the problem,
  where the feedback loop is fast and the win is visible. Expansion rings come after.

### 5. Reach — how the message and product get to them
- **Message:** how, where, and what you say. "Where" has two senses — physically where they are, and
  where their *head* is at.
- **Story type:** is your message a comedy, a drama, an action/adventure, or a **documentary** (evidence-
  led)? Match it to the product and audience.
- **Message basis:** customer intimacy, product innovation, or process excellence — pick the one you
  actually win on and lead with it.
- **Channel:** the concrete route to the customer. The strongest pitches have distribution baked into the
  product itself (it arrives where the work already happens), not bolted on.

### 6. Money / the ask — a clear path
- **How it makes money**, or, for an internal pitch, what the return is (adoption, proof, leverage).
- **Framing the ask.** Real raises are framed over a 12/18/24-month horizon. Internally, the ask is often
  *alignment first, then one proving ground* — not headcount. **Capital is expensive**; spend it to
  expand a model that already works, not to discover whether it works.
- If there's no revenue model yet, say so (`[needs data]`) rather than hand-waving one.

### 7. Team / why win
- **Who has your back?** Why this specific group; what each person uniquely holds. Culture and execution
  beat résumés — but name the credibility you can *honestly* claim.
- **Three sources of advantage** (the intellectual "why win"): typically some mix of a repeatable method
  or hard-won know-how, a structural position competitors can't easily occupy, and distribution. Make
  them concrete.
- **Competition, and the person at the back of the room who says "it's been done."** Acknowledge it —
  usually every *component* exists somewhere. You win by being *first to the assembly*: the specific
  combination, audience, or delivery nobody else has put together. A short competition table (who / what
  they solve / why they don't solve this) is worth a lot.
- **Why anyone cares (the emotional one).** Under the intellectual advantages, there's a human reason this
  should exist. Name it. It's often the line the room remembers.

### The aspiration (the close)
An aspirational goal for team, customers, and partners — the world that exists once this wins. One
sentence, concrete, a little bold. Pairs with the back-of-the-box promise to bookend the pitch.

## Two lenses to run over the whole thing

- **Market trumps all.** However good the product, the size and reachability of the market dominates the
  outcome. If your market sizing is weak or unknown, *say so plainly* — it's usually the highest-value
  thing to go learn, and pretending otherwise fools no one who matters.
- **Altitude.** Keep the vision general and human. Scope, sequencing, and implementation ("Azure first,"
  "internal-only for now") are *roadmap*, not vision — push them down a layer so the top-level story stays
  about the problem and the change, not the machinery.

## A note on honesty as persuasion
The counterintuitive lesson: flagging what you *don't* know (market numbers, the personal why, the
revenue model) and preserving genuine open questions makes a pitch **more** persuasive to serious
audiences, not less. It signals you've thought hard enough to find the edges. Fabricated confidence is
detectable and fatal. This is why `[needs data]` markers are a feature of the method, not a fallback.
